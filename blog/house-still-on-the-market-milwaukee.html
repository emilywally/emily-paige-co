Why is this house still on the market?

In a metro that absorbs listings in nineteen days, a house that sits is telling you something. Here is how to read it.

The text usually arrives late at night, with a link attached. This one has been up for six weeks. Is something wrong with it, or did we just get lucky?

I like this question, because the person asking it is paying attention. They have spent a month losing houses in forty-eight hours, and now something is just sitting there, patient and unclaimed. It feels like a glitch in the system. It is not. It is information.

How long do homes actually take to sell in Milwaukee?

These figures come from the Greater Milwaukee Association of REALTORS® August 2026 report, released September 14 and covering the four-county metro: Milwaukee, Waukesha, Ozaukee and Washington.

Homes went under contract in an average of 19 days in August, unchanged from a year earlier. My own listings average 3 days, which is my figure from Metro MLS rather than an MLS-wide one, and worth saying only because it shows how much of a timeline is set by pricing rather than by the market.

Supply is still thin. GMAR counted 4,991 active listings in August, or 3.4 months of inventory. Strip out the ones that already have an accepted offer and what a buyer can actually walk into is 1.7 months. Six months is considered balanced. The metro average sale price, meanwhile, hit $497,552, up 11.1% year over year, and that is an average rather than a median, so it runs higher than the band most of my clients shop in.

Homes are getting claimed in under three weeks and there is nowhere near enough of them. Against that, a house genuinely available for six weeks is not an oversight. Your job is to find out what everyone else saw.

Why would a house sit in a market this tight?

Nearly always one of three things, and they are not equally fixable.

It is priced above what buyers will pay. The most common reason by a wide margin, and the best one for you. Someone listed at a number that came from a neighbor’s sale, or a renovation receipt, or a feeling. The market declined to agree. A seller six weeks into being wrong is a different negotiator than a seller on day one.

It needs work most buyers cannot take on. Milwaukee’s housing stock is old and wonderful and occasionally expensive. Knob and tube. A roof at the end of its life. A foundation with a story. Most buyers here are stretched to their down payment and have no appetite for a $40,000 surprise, so these homes wait for someone who does. That can be you. Just price the work honestly before you fall for the woodwork, not after.

Something about it is simply fixed. The category people skip, and the one I would most want you to sit with. A house backing a busy road. A second bedroom you reach by walking through the first. A kitchen in the dark back corner with one small window, so you are cooking under electric light at four in the afternoon in January.

Photos hide this well. Wide lenses flatter a cramped layout, and nobody shoots a house on a gray Tuesday. But light and layout are what you cannot renovate your way out of cheaply, and they are exactly what you feel every day. If you tour a sitting listing and leave slightly deflated without being able to say why, take that seriously. The other buyers felt it too. Price and condition are negotiable. This you can only accept or walk away from.

How much below asking can you offer on a house that has been sitting?

People want a percentage. Five under, ten under, some rule they can hold onto. There isn’t one, because the days a listing has been up tell you about the seller, not the house. They tell you how willing someone might be to negotiate. Recent comparable sales tell you what the place is worth. Only the second belongs in your offer.

Work out what similar homes nearby have actually closed at in the last few months, adjust for condition, and offer that. If it lands well under asking, wonderful. If it lands at asking because the home is worth asking and the seller simply had bad luck with timing, an arbitrary discount just costs you a house you wanted.

Read a price cut the same way. It tells you the first price was wrong, not that the new one is right. The pattern is what matters: one correction usually means a seller listening to feedback, while three small cuts over four months means a seller chasing the market down and still standing above it.

And I want to be straight about the hope underneath all of this, because it comes up constantly. People want a sitting listing to mean a bargain everyone else somehow missed. That is rarely the shape of it. More often the discount is real and so is the reason for it, and you are being paid to take on a problem. Sometimes that is a good trade. It is almost never free money.

What should you do before writing the offer?

Tour it twice, at different hours, because light is what photographs lie about most. Pull the full listing history, and know that the days-on-market count on a portal is not always the real one: a listing withdrawn and relisted can look brand new. Get the comparable sales. Keep your inspection contingency and use it hard, because a home that has been sitting is exactly where an inspection earns its fee. Then ask the listing agent why it has not sold. You would be surprised how often someone just tells you.

If you are on the other side of this, watching your own listing pass a third weekend, the fix is almost never more marketing. It is the price, and waiting only makes it more expensive. A listing appointment is how you get the two numbers every other decision depends on, what your home would realistically sell for and what you would walk away with. It is not a commitment to list.

Bring me a listing that has been sitting and I will usually tell you which of the three reasons it is before we go see it. Send it over, or start with the questionnaire if we have not talked yet. About five minutes, and our first conversation starts somewhere useful.

Wondering about the opposite situation, the house with six offers by Sunday? That was last week’s post.

I’d love to hear about it.
