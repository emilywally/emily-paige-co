November 23, 2026 · Field notes

Is it better to buy a house in winter in Milwaukee?

Yes, with one Milwaukee catch: our winter market is November and December, not January and February.

Every year around Thanksgiving, the same question starts showing up in my consultations, and this month it came up more than once. Should we keep looking through the holidays, or pause and start fresh in spring? Do houses really go on sale in winter?

My answer: yes. Houses, in general, are on sale in winter. The catch is that Milwaukee’s market does not follow the calendar. Our winter market is November and December. By the second half of January, spring has already started.

Are houses cheaper in winter in Milwaukee?

In my experience, yes, and the reason is simple: fewer buyers. People are busy with the holidays, the weather is miserable, and plenty of them decide to wait for spring. The sellers still on the market in November and December usually have a reason to be: a job move, a house they have already bought, a listing that has been sitting since fall. Fewer buyers and motivated sellers is where better prices come from.

That does not make it a slow market overall, which is exactly why the window matters. The Greater Milwaukee Association of REALTORS® October 2026 report, released November 13 and covering Milwaukee, Waukesha, Ozaukee and Washington counties, put the average sale price at $460,243, up 5.7% from a year earlier. Sales slipped 3.5%, to 1,493 homes, and 812 of those were in Milwaukee County. Once you set aside homes that already have an accepted offer, the metro had 1.9 months of supply. Six is balanced.

But homes are already taking longer to sell: an average of 27 days on market in October, up from 23 a year earlier. My own listings have averaged 3 days (that is my number, not an MLS-wide one). The difference between those two numbers is mostly price and prep, and in the winter market a buyer gets to take advantage of the homes that missed on one or both.

When is the winter market in Milwaukee?

November and December. Somewhere between January 15 and February 1, depending on the year, the spring market starts. New listings come on, the buyers who waited out the holidays come back, and the competition comes back with them.

So if you are planning to “wait for spring” to shop, know what you are waiting for. Spring here starts in the middle of a Wisconsin January, and it is the busiest stretch of the year. If you want the winter advantage, it is now through the end of December.

Where does the winter discount show up?

In price and in pace. There is more room to offer under asking, especially on a home that has been on the market since September. You get more time to decide, and a much better chance of keeping your inspection contingency. I walk through how I read a listing that has been sitting in why a house is still on the market.

It does not usually show up as extras. Sellers covering your closing costs is still rare, winter or not, and so is a big repair credit after the inspection; expect to talk about safety and major systems, not a punch list. The listings that agree to those things are usually homes that need significant work, or homes priced above the market that have been sitting.

What can you learn about a house in winter that you can’t in May?

This is my favorite part, and it is the one nobody mentions. In winter you get to see a house doing its hardest job.

You can feel whether the furnace keeps up, or whether the back bedroom is ten degrees colder than the rest. You can stand by the original windows in a 1920s bungalow and know what that charm costs in December. Icicles hanging thick along the eaves can point to an attic losing heat. You find out whether the driveway slopes toward the street or toward the garage, which matters a great deal on an icy Tuesday at 7 a.m.

And the light. A kitchen that glows in a June listing photo might get no sun at all by 3:30 in December. If you are going to cook dinner in that room every night, it is worth knowing.

There are tradeoffs. A roof under snow cannot be fully seen, and most inspectors will not run the air conditioning in the cold. Ask for the past year of heating bills, and plan to look at the roof and AC again before closing if you can.

Should I keep looking through the holidays?

If you are pre-approved, yes. This is the window. There will be fewer homes to choose from, so you are trading selection for price and breathing room. Nobody wants to tour a house the week before Christmas, which is more or less the point.

Waiting has its own cost. Freddie Mac had the 30-year fixed at 7.28% the week of November 19, up from 6.26% a year earlier, and no one can tell you where it will be in February. What I can tell you is that by then you will be shopping alongside everyone who waited.

If you are not ready yet, start now rather than after the holidays: a real pre-approval, your must-haves settled, your lease date mapped out. I lay out that timeline in when to start looking for a house. That way, if the right house shows up in December, or the first good listing of the spring market lands in mid-January, you can move that afternoon.

Either way, I’d love to hear where you have landed. Reach out any time, snow or not.

Quick answers

Are houses cheaper in winter in Milwaukee?

Often, yes. In Milwaukee’s winter market, November and December, fewer buyers are shopping and the sellers still listed tend to be motivated, which leaves more room to negotiate on price. The metro market is still tight overall: 1.9 months of supply in October 2026 once homes with accepted offers were set aside, per the Greater Milwaukee Association of REALTORS®. No price outcome is guaranteed.

When does the spring market start in Milwaukee?

Usually between January 15 and February 1, depending on the year. November and December are the slower winter market; from mid-January, new listings and buyer competition pick up.

What should I check when touring a house in winter?

How evenly the house heats, drafts near older windows, heavy icicles or ice along the eaves, how the driveway handles ice, and how much daylight the main rooms get in late afternoon. Ask for a year of heating bills, since snow can hide the roof and air conditioning usually cannot be tested in the cold.
