How does mortgage pre-approval work in Wisconsin?

A plain answer first, then what to bring, how the number gets set, and what changes between a pre-approval and a pre-qualification.

A mortgage pre-approval is a letter from a lender saying that, after reviewing your income, savings, credit and debts, they are prepared to lend you up to a certain amount. In Wisconsin it works the same way as anywhere else: you give a lender your paperwork, they check it, and you get a letter with a loan amount you can attach to an offer.

The letter is not a guarantee that a loan will close. The lender still reviews the specific home you pick, including the bank's check on the home's value, so treat it as strong proof that you are a serious buyer, not a promise.

How much can I get pre-approved for?

The amount comes from three things: your income, your monthly debts and your credit. A lender adds up what you earn, subtracts what you already owe each month on cards, student loans and car payments, and works out how large a payment fits what is left. Your credit history then affects the rate and the loan programs open to you.

Every lender sets its own limits and every loan program has its own rules, so I will not hand you a one size answer here. Your lender will give you the exact number, and the number to care about most is the monthly payment, not the ceiling. Being approved for a larger amount than you are comfortable paying back is common, and it is fine to ask for a letter written for a lower price.

One Wisconsin detail that surprises people: Wisconsin is a marital property state, so if you are married, a lender may count your spouse's debts even when your spouse is not on the loan. Mention that early so the number you get is the real one.

What do I bring to a lender?

Having this ready the first day is the fastest way to get a letter in a day or two instead of a week.

  • Pay stubs from the last 30 days and W-2s from the last two years.
  • Federal tax returns from the last two years (self-employed buyers should expect to need both years, complete).
  • Bank and savings statements, usually the last two months, all pages.
  • A photo ID and your Social Security number so the lender can check your credit.
  • A list of your monthly debts, plus a note explaining any large deposits that are not from your paycheck.
  • If part of your down payment is a gift, a short letter from the person giving it.

Exact lists vary by lender, so ask yours what they want. If you are self-employed, changed jobs recently or have a spouse with separate debts, say so up front, because those are the situations where extra paperwork shows up.

What is the difference between pre-qualification and pre-approval?

Pre-qualification is a rough estimate based on numbers you tell a lender, with nothing checked. Pre-approval means the lender has looked at your actual documents and your credit report. Sellers and their agents treat the two very differently, and a pre-qualification alone makes an offer look softer than one with a real letter attached.

The step after pre-approval is the lender's final sign off on a specific home, once you are under contract. That one is about the home and the loan together, which is why the first letter, strong as it is, is not the last word.

Does pre-approval hurt my credit?

The lender checks your credit report, which can lower your score by a small amount for a short time. Credit scoring generally treats several mortgage checks made close together as a single search, so comparing a few lenders in the same couple of weeks does not need to be scary. Ask your lender how they handle it.

Where should I start in Milwaukee?

Start before you tour homes, not after you fall for one. The letter lets you shop at the right price, and in the Milwaukee area it is often what makes an offer worth a seller's attention. Talk to a couple of lenders, compare the monthly payment each one quotes, and pick the one who answers the phone quickly, because that matters more than you would think once you are under contract.
